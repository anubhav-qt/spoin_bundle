# Twentieth-Century and Contemporary Philosophy: The Analytic and Continental Divide

## The Analytic Tradition: Frege's Logicism and Sense Versus Reference

Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) laid the foundation for modern mathematical logic in his *Begriffsschrift* (1879), replacing traditional Aristotelian subject-predicate syllogistics with function-argument analysis and quantifiers ($\forall, \exists$). Frege pursued **Logicism**—the thesis that arithmetic can be completely reduced to, and derived from, pure formal logic.

In his seminal paper *On Sense and Reference* (*Über Sinn und Bedeutung*, 1892), Frege resolved the semantic puzzle of identity statements:
- Why does the true identity statement "The Morning Star is the Morning Star" ($a = a$) express an uninformative cognitive tautology, whereas "The Morning Star is the Evening Star" ($a = b$) represents an informative astronomical discovery?
- Frege demonstrated that a linguistic sign possesses two distinct semantic dimensions:
  1. **Reference (*Bedeutung*):** The actual object or referent in the physical world that the sign designates. Both "The Morning Star" (*Phosphorus*) and "The Evening Star" (*Hesperus*) share the exact same reference: the planet Venus.
  2. **Sense (*Sinn*):** The mode of presentation of the object—the specific cognitive perspective or conceptual route through which the reference is identified. "The celestial body visible in the dawn" conveys a completely different sense from "the celestial body visible at twilight."

Because the senses differ, identity statements linking them convey genuine cognitive content without asserting that two different objects are identical.

## Bertrand Russell: Descriptions and Logical Atomism

Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) discovered **Russell's Paradox** in Frege's naive set theory: consider the set $R$ of all sets that do not contain themselves. Does $R$ contain itself? If $R$ contains itself, it violates its definition and must not contain itself; if $R$ does not contain itself, it must contain itself. This antinomy demonstrated that unrestricted set comprehension is mathematically inconsistent, prompting Russell's Type Theory and Zermelo-Fraenkel set axiomatization.

In *On Denoting* (1905), Russell formulated the **Theory of Definite Descriptions**, solving the metaphysical puzzle of non-existent referents (such as "The present King of France is bald"):
- Traditional philosophers (like Alexius Meinong) argued that because the sentence is meaningful, there must exist a shadowy metaphysical entity called "The present King of France" that possesses the property of being non-existent.
- Russell demonstrated that ordinary grammatical surface structure obscures logical deep structure. The proposition "The present King of France is bald" is not a simple subject-predicate assertion, but an existential conjunction of three claims:
  1. *Existence:* There exists an $x$ such that $x$ is currently King of France ($\exists x (Kx)$).
  2. *Uniqueness:* For any $y$, if $y$ is currently King of France, then $y = x$ ($\forall y (Ky \rightarrow y = x)$).
  3. *Predication:* $x$ is bald ($Bx$).
- Because condition (1) is empirically false, the entire compound proposition is simply false, without requiring non-existent entities to populate reality.

## Ludwig Wittgenstein: From the Tractatus to the Philosophical Investigations

Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) radically transformed analytic philosophy across two distinct historical phases:

### The Early Phase: *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921)
- **The Picture Theory of Language:** Language functions by producing logical pictures of reality. "The world is the totality of facts, not of things" (1.1). A proposition is meaningful if its constituent logical structure mirrors a possible atomic state of affairs in the world.
- **The Limits of Language:** Propositions can only state factual states of affairs in empirical nature. Metaphysics, ethics, and aesthetics belong to what cannot be stated; they cannot be said (*sagen*), but only shown (*zeigen*).
- **Proposition 7:** *"Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent"* (*Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muss man schweigen*).

### The Later Phase: *Philosophical Investigations* (*Philosophische Untersuchungen*, 1953)
- Wittgenstein repudiated the rigid logical essentialism of the *Tractatus*.
- **Meaning as Use:** For a vast class of linguistic cases, the meaning of a word is not an abstract object it points to; meaning is its practical **use in the language** (§43).
- **Language Games (*Sprachspiele*):** Language is an open-ended collection of human practices and social activities (giving commands, telling jokes, praying, describing), interwoven into human **Forms of Life** (*Lebensformen*).
- **Family Resemblance (*Familienähnlichkeit*):** Concepts (such as "game") do not possess a single, universal essence shared by all instances. Rather, instances are connected by a network of overlapping similarities, like physical resemblances between members of a biological family.
- **The Private Language Argument (§§243–315):** A language that refers exclusively to private, internal sensory sensations inaccessible to anyone else is conceptually impossible. Rule-following requires an objective, social criterion to distinguish between "following a rule" and "merely believing one is following a rule."

## The Continental Tradition: Husserl's Phenomenology and Heidegger's Dasein

Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) founded **Phenomenology** under the rallying cry: *"Back to the things themselves!"* (*Zu den Sachen selbst!*). Husserl argued that consciousness is characterized by **Intentionality**—all consciousness is always directed toward an object (*consciousness of* something). To uncover the pure structures of conscious experience, Husserl introduced the **Epoché** (Phenomenological Reduction): systematically bracketing or suspending the "natural attitude" (our unexamined assumption of the independent existence of the external physical world) to directly describe how phenomena present themselves to the transcendental ego.

Martin Heidegger (1889–1976), in *Being and Time* (*Sein und Zeit*, 1927), radicalized phenomenology into **Fundamental Ontology**:
- Heidegger claimed that Western philosophy had forgotten the foundational Question of Being (**Seinsfrage**), mistaking entities for Being itself.
- **Dasein ("Being-there"):** Heidegger designated the human entity as *Dasein*, the being for whom its own Being is an issue.
- **Being-in-the-World (*In-der-Welt-sein*):** Humans are not detached Cartesian minds contemplating an external world; we are always already embedded in a pre-reflective web of practical concerns and equipment. Entities are primarily encountered not as passive objects of theoretical gaze (*Vorhandenheit* / present-at-hand), but as tools ready-for-use (**Zuhandenheit** / ready-to-hand), like a hammer in skilled craftsmanship.
- **Thrownness (*Geworfenheit*):** Dasein always finds itself already "thrown" into a specific historical culture, language, and biological condition.
- **Das Man ("The They"):** The anonymous social conformism of everyday public life, wherein individuals think, judge, and act as "one" does, escaping personal responsibility in **Inauthenticity** (*Uneigentlichkeit*).
- **Being-toward-Death (*Sein-zum-Tode*):** Death is the non-relational, unsurpassable possibility of Dasein. Confronting mortality through deep existential anxiety (**Angst**) shatters conformist delusions, enabling **Authenticity** (*Eigentlichkeit*).

## French Existentialism: Jean-Paul Sartre and Bad Faith

In post-WWII France, existentialism addressed human freedom, alienation, and radical responsibility through the philosophy of Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980):
- Core Axiom: **"Existence precedes essence"** (*L'existence précède l'essence*). Unlike an artifact (like a paper knife, whose blueprint/essence precedes its manufacture), a human being exists first, encounters themselves in the world, and only afterward defines their essence through their free choices and actions.
- Human Ontology (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943):
  - *Être-en-soi* (Being-in-itself): Non-conscious material objects with fixed, immutable essences.
  - *Être-pour-soi* (Being-for-itself): Conscious human subjectivity, characterized by radical freedom and internal nothingness (*le néant*).
- Because there is no God or human nature to provide external excuses, humans are **"condemned to be free."**
- **Bad Faith (*Mauvaise Foi*):** The psychological self-deception wherein a free subject pretends to be a fixed object (in-itself), evading the anxiety of freedom. Sartre illustrated this via the café waiter whose rigid, exaggerated movements perform a social role like an automaton to evade his existential freedom.

## Existential Ethics and the Absurd: Simone de Beauvoir and Albert Camus

Extending beyond individual subjectivity, French existentialism developed social ethics and confronted the confrontation with an indifferent cosmos:

**Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986):**
- In *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), de Beauvoir demonstrated that existentialism yields a rigorous ethics: human beings are caught between *facticity* (material constraints) and *transcendence* (freedom to project future goals). One cannot authentically will one's own freedom without actively willing and defending the freedom of others.
- In *The Second Sex* (*Le Deuxième Sexe*, 1949), de Beauvoir founded modern feminist philosophy with the declaration: *"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman"* (*On ne naît pas femme: on le devient*). She dissected how patriarchal civilization constructs woman as the essentialized, subordinate "Other" (*l'Autre*) relative to the normative male subject.

**Albert Camus (1913–1960):**
- In *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), Camus defined **The Absurd** as the unbridgeable collision between the human demand for purpose, unity, and meaning, and the cold, unreasonable silence of an indifferent universe.
- Camus declared that suicide is an evasion of the absurd. The authentic response is **Revolt**: living with defiance, passion, and lucid consciousness of meaninglessness. Sisyphus, eternally rolling his stone up the mountain only to watch it plunge downward, is the absurd hero: *"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."*
