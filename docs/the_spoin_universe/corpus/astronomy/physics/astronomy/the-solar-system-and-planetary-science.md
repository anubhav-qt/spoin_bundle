# The Solar System and Planetary Science

Planetary science investigates the formation, geophysical differentiation, atmospheric evolution, and orbital distribution of celestial bodies within gravitationally bound star systems. The Solar System serves as the empirical archetype for comparative planetology, characterized by distinct thermal condensation zones, sharply partitioned planetary classes, and vast peripheral reservoirs of primordial icy planetesimals.

## The Nebular Hypothesis and the Frost Line

The Solar System formed approximately $4.567\text{ billion years ago}$ from the gravitational collapse of a fragmented portion of a giant interstellar molecular cloud composed primarily of hydrogen and helium enriched with heavier stellar nucleosynthetic debris.

As the pre-solar nebula collapsed, conservation of angular momentum caused the cloud to spin faster, flattening it into a circumstellar protoplanetary disk orbiting a central protostar. Within this disk, temperatures decreased with radial distance from the proto-Sun, establishing a critical geochemical boundary known as the **Frost Line** (or Snow Line) at approximately $2.7\text{ AU}$ (between modern Mars and Jupiter):

- **Interior to the Frost Line ($T > 170\text{ K}$):** Temperatures were too high for volatile compounds (water, methane, ammonia) to freeze. Only refractory materials with high condensation temperatures—silicate minerals, nickel, and iron—could condense into solid grains. These rocky grains collided and grew via electrostatic and gravitational accretion into planetesimals, forming the dense, rocky **Terrestrial Planets**. Because refractory elements comprised less than 1% of the nebular mass, terrestrial planet masses remained small.
- **Exterior to the Frost Line ($T < 170\text{ K}$):** Volatile ices condensed in vast quantities alongside silicates. Icy planetesimals rapidly accreted into massive proto-planetary cores exceeding 10 Earth masses ($10 M_\oplus$). These massive cores exerted sufficient gravitational attraction to trigger runaway hydrodynamic capture of surrounding nebular hydrogen and helium gas, forming the massive **Gas Giants**.

## Terrestrial Planet Geophysics and Comparative Atmospheres

The four inner terrestrial planets—Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars—exhibit high mean bulk densities ($3.9 - 5.5\text{ g/cm}^3$) and internal radial differentiation resulting from early radioactive and gravitational melting:

- **Internal Differentiation:** Heavy metallic elements (primarily iron and nickel) sank to the planetary center to form dense cores, while lighter silicate compounds floated upward to form convective mantles and solid surface crusts. Convective circulation within molten metallic outer cores generates global dipolar magnetic fields via planetary dynamos (robust on Earth, weak on Mercury, absent on Venus and Mars).
- **Secondary Atmospheric Evolution:** The primary hydrogen-helium atmospheres were lost due to low escape velocities and intense early solar T-Tauri winds. Subsequent atmospheres were generated via volcanic outgassing (emitting $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{SO}_2$, and $\text{N}_2$) and cometary impacts.
- **Runaway Greenhouse on Venus:** While Earth established a balanced carbon-silicate cycle that sequestered carbon dioxide into carbonate rocks via liquid water oceans, Venus experienced a catastrophic runaway greenhouse effect. Solar irradiance boiled away early surface water; without oceans to absorb $\text{CO}_2$, atmospheric carbon dioxide accumulated to $96.5\%$ at a crushing surface pressure of 92 atmospheres. The resulting infrared opacity traps thermal radiation, maintaining a uniform surface temperature of $737\text{ K}$ ($464^\circ\text{C}$), hotter than the daytime surface of Mercury.
- **Atmospheric Stripping on Mars:** With only $0.107 M_\oplus$, Mars cooled rapidly, causing its core dynamo to freeze. Deprived of a protective global magnetosphere, solar wind plasma stripped the Martian atmosphere over billions of years, reducing surface pressure to $0.006\text{ atm}$ and desiccating its surface water.

## Jovian Gas Giants and Ice Giants

The outer planets partition into two distinct structural classifications based on mass and internal composition:

1. **Gas Giants (Jupiter and Saturn):**
   - Composed overwhelmingly of hydrogen (~75%) and helium (~24%), closely mirroring the solar composition.
   - Jupiter ($317.8 M_\oplus$) and Saturn ($95.2 M_\oplus$) possess central rocky/icy cores enveloped by deep convective envelopes.
   - At internal depths where hydrostatic pressure exceeds $200\text{ GPa}$ (2 million atmospheres), molecular hydrogen ($H_2$) transitions into a high-density, degenerate phase known as **liquid metallic hydrogen**. The delocalized electrons of metallic hydrogen conduct electricity efficiently; rapid planetary rotation drives powerful convective dynamo loops, generating gargantuan magnetospheres (Jupiter's magnetic moment is 20,000 times that of Earth).
2. **Ice Giants (Uranus and Neptune):**
   - Smaller masses (Uranus $14.5 M_\oplus$, Neptune $17.1 M_\oplus$) and higher bulk densities ($1.27 - 1.64\text{ g/cm}^3$) than pure gas giants.
   - Possess thin outer hydrogen-helium atmospheres (10–20% of total mass) overlying vast, high-pressure, supercritical fluid mantles composed of "ices"—water, methane, and ammonia.
   - Trace atmospheric methane absorbs red optical wavelengths, reflecting vibrant blue and cyan light.
   - Both planets exhibit highly tilted, non-dipolar, offset magnetic fields generated not in a central core, but through thin convective outer ionic fluid shells.

## Minor Body Reservoirs: Asteroids, Kuiper Belt, and Comets

Remnants of primordial planetesimals remain preserved across three major astronomical reservoirs:

- **The Main Asteroid Belt (2.2 to 3.2 AU):** Occupies the orbital gap between Mars and Jupiter. Planetesimal accretion was arrested by Jupiter's strong gravitational perturbations. Periodic orbital resonances with Jupiter clear distinct orbital gaps (known as **Kirkwood Gaps**). The largest object, the dwarf planet Ceres, contains one-third of the belt's total mass.
- **The Kuiper Belt (30 to 50 AU):** A circumstellar disc of icy debris situated beyond Neptune's orbit. Kuiper Belt Objects (KBOs) are primitive mixtures of rock, water ice, frozen methane, and nitrogen. Gravitational resonances with Neptune govern population subgroups, such as the Plutinos locked in a $2:3$ orbital resonance with Neptune.
- **The Oort Cloud (2,000 to 100,000 AU):** An isotropic, spherical theoretical reservoir of trillions of icy planetesimals extending nearly halfway to the nearest stars. Formed from planetesimals gravitationally scattered outward by giant planet migration, the Oort cloud is the primary source of long-period comets ($P > 200\text{ years}$).
- **Cometary Morphology:** Comets are volatile-rich planetesimals. Inside $3\text{ AU}$, solar radiation sublimates surface ices, forming an expansive gas **coma**. Radiation pressure and the solar wind separate outgassed material into two distinct tails:
  - *The Dust Tail:* Curved and yellowish, pushed by solar photon radiation pressure.
  - *The Ion (Gas) Tail:* Linear and bluish (dominated by $\text{CO}^+$ fluorescence), channeled directly away from the Sun along interplanetary magnetic field lines.

## The IAU 2006 Planet Definition and Dwarf Planet Criteria

The discovery in the early 2000s of massive trans-Neptunian bodies—particularly Eris, an object of comparable mass to Pluto—prompted the International Astronomical Union (IAU) to formalize the astronomical definition of a planet. Under **IAU Resolution 5A (2006)**, a celestial body is classified as a **Planet** if and only if it satisfies three criteria:

1. It is in orbit around the Sun.
2. It possesses sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape.
3. It has **cleared the neighborhood** around its orbit.

The third criterion requires a planet to be the gravitationally dominant body along its orbital zone, having accreted, ejected, or gravitationally locked surrounding planetesimals.

Bodies that meet criteria 1 and 2 but fail criterion 3 (and are not natural satellites) are classified as **Dwarf Planets**. Pluto shares its orbital zone with thousands of trans-Neptunian Kuiper Belt objects; its orbit crosses that of Neptune, demonstrating that it does not dynamically dominate its zone. Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake are officially designated dwarf planets under this taxonomic framework.
