# Hellenistic and Late Antique Philosophy: Stoicism, Epicureanism, Pyrrhonism, and Neoplatonism

## The Stoic System: Physics, Logic, and the Logos

Founded by Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE) around 300 BCE at the *Stoa Poikile* (Painted Porch) in Athens, Stoicism was systematized by Chrysippus of Soli and transmitted to the Roman world by Seneca the Younger, Epictetus, and Emperor Marcus Aurelius. Stoicism integrated logic, physics, and ethics into a mutually interdependent, organic system.

In physics, the Stoics were pantheistic materialists. They maintained that only material bodies can act or be acted upon. The cosmos is a single, living, rational organism permeated by an active physical principle: the *Logos* (divine cosmic reason) or *Pneuma* (a warm, dynamic breath composed of active fire and air). The *Logos* acts upon passive matter (*hyle*) to determine the structure, cohesion, and cyclical evolution of the universe through providential causal determinism (*heimarmene*). At the close of each cosmic cycle, the universe resolves into fire through universal conflagration (*ekpyrosis*), after which it reconstitutes in an identical cycle.

Stoic logic developed propositional calculus (analyzing conditional, disjunctive, and conjunctive syllogisms) and formulated an empirical epistemology based on the *kataleptic impression* (*phantasia kataleptike*)—a cognitive impression so clear and distinct that it commands the rational intellect's immediate assent (*synkatathesis*), providing an infallible criterion of empirical truth.

## Stoic Ethics: The Dichotomy of Control, Indifferents, and Apatheia

The foundational axiom of Stoic ethics is that **Virtue is the sole good** (*monon to kalon agathon*) and vice the sole evil. Virtue consists in living in accordance with Nature (*homologoumenos te physei zen*)—which means living in accordance with human reason, our share of the cosmic *Logos*. Virtue is all-or-nothing and sufficient for happiness (*eudaimonia*).

External entities—such as physical health, wealth, social standing, reputation, and life itself—are classified as **Indifferents** (*adiaphora*). They possess no intrinsic moral value and cannot make a human life morally better or worse. However, the Stoics distinguished between:
- *Preferred Indifferents* (*proegmena*): Natural conditions such as health, adequate shelter, and material security that have value (*axia*) and should be reasonably chosen when virtue is not compromised.
- *Dispreferred Indifferents* (*apoproegmena*): Unfavorable states such as sickness, poverty, and physical injury that have disvalue and should be avoided, but do not impair moral flourishing.

Epictetus (c. 50–135 CE) formulated the operational core of Stoic practice in the **Dichotomy of Control** (*Enchiridion* 1.1):
- *Things in our control* (*ta eph' hemin*): Opinions, impulses to act, desires, aversions—the internal choices of the rational will (*prohairesis*).
- *Things outside our control* (*ta ouk eph' hemin*): The physical body, property, family, reputation, public honors, and external outcomes.

Psychological distress arises exclusively from attributing moral value to things outside one's control or demanding that reality conform to personal desire. By exercising assent over impressions, the Stoic achieves **Apatheia**—freedom from irrational, destructive passions (*pathe*—such as rage, grief, and fear). *Apatheia* is not emotional numbness or psychopathic apathy, but the cultivation of rational, healthy emotional states (*eupatheiai*: joy, moral caution, and benevolence). This culminates in **Amor Fati** (love of one's fate) and **Cosmopolitanism**—recognizing every human being as an equal citizen of the universal cosmic polis (*kosmopolites*).

## Epicureanism: Materialist Atomism and the Garden

Founded by Epicurus (341–270 BCE) around 306 BCE at "The Garden" (*Kepos*) outside Athens, Epicureanism offered a competing therapeutic philosophy centered on materialist atomism and enlightened hedonism. Uniquely for antiquity, the Garden opened its community to women, enslaved persons, and non-citizens.

Epicurean physics was inherited from Democritus and Leucippus. The cosmos consists entirely of infinite indivisible particles (**atoms**, *atoma*) moving through infinite **void** (*kenon*). Nothing is created from nothing, and nothing is annihilated into non-existence. To preserve human agency within physical determinism, Epicurus postulated the **Atomic Swerve** (*clinamen*): an uncaused, indeterminate deviation of an atom from its straight downward trajectory at no fixed time or location. The soul is a material organ made of exceptionally fine atoms that disperse entirely upon physical death.

Because the soul dissolves at bodily death, consciousness ceases. Epicurus deduced that death cannot harm the deceased: *"Death is nothing to us. When we exist, death is not; and when death exists, we are not."* Furthermore, while the gods exist as immortal, blissful atomic beings in the spaces between worlds (*intermundia*), they exert no influence over physical nature, meteors, or human fate, and do not punish mortals. The Roman poet Lucretius systematically articulated this atomistic physics and anti-superstitious therapy in *De Rerum Natura* (On the Nature of Things, c. 50 BCE).

## Epicurean Hedonism: Aponia, Ataraxia, and the Tetrapharmakos

Epicurus identified **Pleasure** (*hedone*) as the primary and intrinsic good, the ultimate end (*telos*) of life. However, Epicureanism defined pleasure negatively rather than as active sensory indulgence:
- **Aponia:** The absence of physical pain in the body.
- **Ataraxia:** The absence of anxiety, mental distress, and existential dread in the soul (unshakable tranquility).

Epicurus distinguished between *kinetic pleasure* (the active stimulation experienced while satisfying an active desire, such as eating a rich feast) and *katastematic pleasure* (the stable, enduring state of equilibrium experienced once lack and pain have been removed). Katastematic pleasure represents the natural limit of pleasure; physical indulgence cannot increase it and often produces future physical pain and anxiety.

Epicurus classified human desires into three strict categories:
1. **Natural and Necessary Desires:** Basic nutrition, water, simple shelter, safety, friendship, and philosophical contemplation. These are easily satisfied and remove bodily pain.
2. **Natural but Unnecessary Desires:** Exquisite culinary delicacies, fine wine, luxury apparel, and sexual indulgence. These vary sensory pleasure without removing basic pain and risk habituation.
3. **Unnatural and Unnecessary (Vain/Empty) Desires:** Political power, fame, military honors, and infinite wealth. These are unlimited by nature, depend on external validation, and generate ceaseless anxiety.

Epicurean ethics summarized its psychological cure in the **Tetrapharmakos** (The Four-Part Remedy):
1. God is nothing to fear;
2. Death is nothing to worry about;
3. What is good is easy to obtain;
4. What is terrible is easy to endure.

Epicureans rejected civic political ambition (*Lathe biosas*—"Live unnoticed"), maintaining that philosophical **Friendship** (*philia*) in a private community provides the highest social security and psychological joy.

## Pyrrhonism: Equipollence, Epoché, and Ataraxia

Ancient Skepticism developed in two rival traditions: the Academic Skepticism of Plato's Middle Academy (Arcesilaus and Carneades, who attacked Stoic dogmatism and defended probabilism, *to pithanon*) and **Pyrrhonism**, initiated by Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE) and documented by Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE) in *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*.

Sextus Empiricus classified philosophical schools into three orientations:
- *Dogmatists* (such as Aristotle and the Stoics) claim to have discovered the truth.
- *Negative Dogmatists* (Academic skeptics) claim that the truth cannot be known by humans.
- *Skeptics (Pyrrhonists)* continue to investigate without asserting truth or dogmatic impossibility.

The Pyrrhonist method begins by setting contradictory arguments, sensory perceptions, and appearances into opposition (*isostheneia*—the equal persuasive weight or equipollence of opposing claims). Unable to determine which side is objectively true, the investigator experiences **Epoché** (suspension of judgment). Unexpectedly, upon suspending judgment, the investigator is immediately released from anxiety, experiencing **Ataraxia** (mental tranquility)—"like a shadow following a body." The Pyrrhonist does not deny daily appearances; they navigate practical life guided by natural inclinations, bodily appetites, social customs, and technical crafts, but without dogmatic belief (*adoxastos*).

## Agrippa's Five Modes and the Epistemological Trilemma

Agrippa the Skeptic formulated **Agrippa's Five Modes** of doubt, which establish the foundational epistemological trilemma against any claim to justified truth:

1. **Disagreement (*Diaphonia*):** Irreconcilable disputes exist among philosophers and laypeople concerning any given matter.
2. **Infinite Regress:** Any premise offered to justify a proposition requires a prior justification, which in turn requires another, ad infinitum.
3. **Relativity:** Objects appear differently depending on the observer, the sensory organ, physical distance, and environmental medium.
4. **Hypothesis (Dogmatic Assumption):** To halt infinite regress, dogmatists arbitrarily assert an unproven premise as an absolute axiom.
5. **Circularity (*Diallelus*):** The premise used to prove a conclusion relies directly or indirectly on the conclusion itself for its own validity.

Because any dogmatic justification must fall into infinite regress, arbitrary circularity, or dogmatic assumption, absolute epistemic certainty remains unattainable.

## Neoplatonism: Plotinus and the Emanation of the One

In late antiquity, Plotinus (c. 204/5–270 CE) synthesized Platonic metaphysics, Aristotelian ontology, and Stoic cosmology into the school later termed **Neoplatonism**, recorded by his student Porphyry in the *Enneads*.

Plotinus rejected dualistic and materialist ontologies, establishing a hierarchical monism structured across three primary hypostases (metaphysical realities):

1. **The One (*To Hen*) / The Good:** The supreme, ineffable, transcendent first principle. The One is utterly simple, indivisible, and beyond Being and thought itself. The One does not create through deliberative will or temporal act; rather, all reality overflows or **emanates** (*aporrhoia*) from its infinite superabundance, analogous to light radiating eternally from the sun without diminishing its source.
2. **Intellect (*Nous*):** The first emanation from the One. As Intellect turns to gaze upon the One, it experiences duality (the thinker and the thought) and becomes the realm of True Being, containing within itself all Platonic Forms as living, interconnected thoughts.
3. **Soul (*Psyche*):** The second emanation, radiating from *Nous*. Soul bridges the intelligible realm and the material world. It is subdivided into the higher World Soul (*Anima Mundi*) and individual human souls. Soul generates time through its discursive, linear movement.

At the lowest periphery of this emanation lies **Matter** (*hyle*)—not an active evil force, but the total privation of light, order, and form. Human salvation (*henosis*—mystical unification with the One) is achieved by purifying the soul from somatic sensory entanglements, cultivating moral virtue, ascending through philosophical contemplation from Soul to Intellect, and ultimately surrendering discursive thought to merge with the One in ecstatic intellectual union.
