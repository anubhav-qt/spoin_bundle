## Vector Algebra and Coordinate Transformations
Linear algebra constitutes the universal mathematical language of spatial game development. Two-dimensional and three-dimensional vectors quantify physical quantities including world positions, velocities, linear accelerations, surface normals, and directional headings. The Dot Product ($\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = |\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\cos\theta = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z$) is ubiquitous across graphics and gameplay systems: computing the dot product between a character's forward heading vector and a directional vector to an enemy determines whether the enemy lies within the character's field of view ($\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} > 0$), while in shading models, the dot product between a surface normal and a light vector ($\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L}$) establishes Lambertian diffuse illumination. The Cross Product ($\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B}$), defined exclusively in three dimensions, yields a vector orthogonal to both input vectors, serving as the foundational tool for computing surface normals of polygon triangles, calculating angular momentum and physical torque, and deriving the orthonormal camera basis vectors (Right, Up, Forward). Spatial transformations are encapsulated within $4 \times 4$ homogeneous transformation matrices. By adopting four-dimensional coordinates $(x, y, z, w)$—where $w=1$ represents physical spatial points subject to translation, and $w=0$ represents pure directional vectors immune to translation—engines chain scaling, rotation, and translation into a single unified matrix. Real-time rendering pipelines pipe vertex data through an affine matrix chain: the Model Matrix converts local object coordinates to world space, the View Matrix re-orients the world relative to the camera's eye, and the Projection Matrix maps the camera view volume into Normalized Device Coordinates (NDC) via perspective division.

## Rotations in 3D: Euler Angles, Gimbal Lock, and Quaternions
Representing three-dimensional angular orientations poses one of the most critical challenges in engine mathematics. The most intuitive representation, Euler Angles, decomposes orientation into three sequential axial rotations: Pitch, Yaw, and Roll. However, Euler angles suffer from severe mathematical defects: their resulting orientation is strictly order-dependent (rotating around $X$ then $Y$ then $Z$ produces a different physical orientation than $Z$ then $Y$ then $X$), and they inevitably encounter Gimbal Lock. Gimbal lock is the catastrophic loss of one rotational degree of freedom that occurs when two of the three rotation axes align in parallel (such as pitching a camera directly upwards by 90 degrees, which causes roll and yaw to manipulate the exact same physical axis). To achieve robust, artifact-free 3D rotations, modern game engines adopt Unit Quaternions. A quaternion is a four-dimensional hypercomplex number of the form $q = w + xi + yj + zk$, where $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$. Geometrically, a unit quaternion encodes an arbitrary spatial rotation through an angle $\theta$ around a three-dimensional unit vector axis $\mathbf{v}$ using the compact formulation $q = [\cos(\theta/2), \sin(\theta/2)\mathbf{v}]$. Quaternions are completely immune to gimbal lock, require only four floating-point numbers in memory compared to the sixteen required by a transformation matrix, and can be normalized efficiently via the constraint $|q| = 1$. Furthermore, quaternions enable Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP), smoothly and realistically blending between two orientations along the shortest geodesic arc of a 4D hypersphere with constant angular velocity.

## Collision Detection: Broad-Phase Spatial Partitioning
Real-time physics engines subdivide the computationally expensive task of collision detection into two distinct hierarchical stages: the Broad-Phase and the Narrow-Phase. In a virtual scene containing $N$ dynamic entities, testing every geometry directly against every other geometry results in an unmanageable quadratic complexity of $O(N^2)$. Testing 10,000 entities naively would require 50 million geometric intersection checks every single frame. The broad-phase eliminates this bottleneck by discarding distant pairs using rapid, conservative approximations, specifically Axis-Aligned Bounding Boxes (AABBs)—the smallest non-rotated rectangular boxes that fully enclose an object's complex geometry. Modern broad-phase architectures organize these AABBs into spatial acceleration structures. In Bounding Volume Hierarchies (BVH) and Dynamic AABB Trees (the standard in Box2D, PhysX, and Jolt Physics), objects are arranged in a hierarchical binary tree where leaf nodes hold entity colliders and internal nodes hold enclosing bounding boxes; raycasts and moving colliders traverse the tree in $O(\log N)$ time. Alternative broad-phase strategies include Spatial Hashing, which maps 2D or 3D world space into a discrete uniform grid and places entities into hash-table buckets based on grid cell occupancy, and Sweep and Prune (SAP), which sorts AABB coordinate intervals along world axes to identify bounding box overlaps with minimal computational overhead.

## Narrow-Phase Collision Algorithms: SAT, GJK, and EPA
Once the broad-phase identifies a candidate pair of potentially overlapping bounding volumes, the Narrow-Phase executes exact geometric algorithms to confirm whether physical penetration exists and to calculate the contact manifold (contact points, penetration depth, and normal vector) required by the physics solver. For convex polygonal shapes in 2D engines like Box2D, the Separating Axis Theorem (SAT) serves as the standard algorithm. SAT states that two convex polyhedra do not intersect if there exists at least one line or axis onto which their projections do not overlap. The engine projects the vertices of both shapes onto candidate axes—formed by the edge normals of both polygons—and checks for intervals of separation; if any separating axis is discovered, the shapes are non-colliding, and if no separation exists on any axis, the axis with the minimum projection overlap defines the penetration depth and normal. In complex 3D environments, engines utilize the Gilbert-Johnson-Keerthi (GJK) algorithm. GJK operates by evaluating whether the origin of the coordinate system is contained within the Minkowski Difference ($A \ominus B = \{a - b \mid a \in A, b \in B\}$) of the two convex geometries. By iteratively querying the geometric "support mapping" (the furthest point along an arbitrary direction), GJK constructs a geometric simplex (a point, line segment, triangle, or tetrahedron) to determine containment in logarithmic iterations. When GJK detects penetration, the Expanding Polytope Algorithm (EPA) expands the internal simplex outward until it touches the boundary of the Minkowski Difference, extracting the precise minimum translation vector needed to resolve the collision.

## Rigid Body Dynamics, Numerical Integration, and Solvers
Real-time physical simulation translates collision detection data into dynamic motion through rigid body dynamics. A rigid body possesses physical properties including total mass $m$, center of mass, moment of inertia tensor $\mathbf{I}$, spatial position $\mathbf{x}$, linear velocity $\mathbf{v}$, rotational quaternion $q$, and angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. To advance these kinematic states through time, engines use numerical integration. Standard Explicit Euler integration ($\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_t \Delta t$; $\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \mathbf{v}_t + \mathbf{a}_t \Delta t$) is notoriously unstable in games because it artificially injects mechanical energy into the simulation, causing stacks of resting boxes to violently oscillate and explode. Professional physics engines implement Symplectic or Semi-Implicit Euler integration, updating velocity prior to integrating position ($\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \mathbf{v}_t + \mathbf{a}_t \Delta t$; $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_{t+1} \Delta t$), which conserves energy in Hamiltonian dynamical systems. Physical constraints—such as non-penetration contacts, frictional resistance, hinges, and ball-and-socket joints—are resolved using iterative constraint solvers. Pioneered by Erin Catto in Box2D and adopted widely in 3D engines, the Sequential Impulse method solves velocity constraints iteratively using Projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS), applying directional impulse pairs to colliding bodies to eliminate relative penetration velocity while enforcing Coulomb friction cones. For high-velocity objects such as bullets or racing vehicles, Continuous Collision Detection (CCD) sweeps colliders through space or calculates exact Time-of-Impact (TOI) sub-steps, preventing thin colliders from tunneling through walls.
