# Cardiovascular Physiology and Hemodynamics: Electrophysiology, The Cardiac Cycle, Vascular Resistance, and Hemostasis

The human cardiovascular system is a closed dual-circuit hydraulic loop that circulates oxygenated blood, nutrients, hormones, and immune factors through systemic tissues while directing deoxygenated blood to the pulmonary vascular bed for gas exchange. The muscular heart serves as a four-chambered electromechanical pump generating convective hydrostatic pressure gradients that overcome systemic peripheral vascular resistance.

## Functional Cardiac Anatomy and Structural Histology

The adult human heart resides within the middle mediastinum, enveloped by the fibrous and serous pericardial sac. It is anatomically divided into four distinct chambers: the thin-walled **Right and Left Atria** (capacitance chambers that collect venous return) and the thick-walled **Right and Left Ventricles** (muscular pressure chambers that eject blood into the pulmonary artery and systemic aorta, respectively). 

Because the left ventricle must generate sufficient hydrostatic pressure to overcome high systemic vascular resistance (~80–120 mmHg) compared to low pulmonary vascular resistance (~10–25 mmHg), the left ventricular myocardial wall is three times thicker than that of the right ventricle.

Unidirectional blood flow is enforced by four mechanical valves that open and close passively in response to transvalvular pressure gradients:
- **Atrioventricular (AV) Valves:** Prevent backflow from ventricles to atria during ventricular systole. The **Tricuspid Valve** (three leaflets) guards the right AV orifice; the **Mitral (Bicuspid) Valve** (two leaflets) guards the left AV orifice. Valve leaflets are anchored to ventricular papillary muscles via fibrous chordae tendineae, preventing leaflet eversion (prolapse) under systolic pressure.
- **Semilunar Valves:** Prevent retrograde flow from the great arteries into the relaxing ventricles during diastole. The **Aortic Valve** guards the left ventricular outflow tract; the **Pulmonary Valve** guards the right ventricular outflow tract. Each consists of three fibrous, pocket-like crescent cusps.

At the microscopic level, cardiac myocytes are branched, striated, mono- or binucleated cells interconnected end-to-end at specialized structural junctions termed **Intercalated Discs**. Intercalated discs contain two specialized junctions:
1. *Fascia Adherens and Desmosomes:* Provide mechanical anchorage, transferring contractile tensile force from one myocyte to the next.
2. *Gap Junctions (Connexons composed of Connexin-43):* Provide low-resistance electrical conduits permitting rapid, direct intercellular passage of inorganic ions ($Na^+, Ca^{2+}$). This electrical coupling enables the entire myocardium to contract synchronously as a functional syncytium.

## Cardiac Electrophysiology: Pacemaker Cells Versus Working Myocytes

Cardiac electrical activation originates within specialized autorhythmic pacemaker tissue and propagates rapidly through a specialized conduction network:

1. **The Sinoatrial (SA) Node Action Potential:** Situated subepicardially at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium, the SA node acts as the primary physiological pacemaker (intrinsic automaticity: 60 to 100 depolarizations per minute). SA node cells lack a true resting potential, displaying spontaneous **Phase 4 Diastolic Depolarization**:
   - Spontaneous depolarization is driven by the inward **Funny Current ($I_f$)**, carried predominantly by $Na^+$ entering via hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN4) channels that open when the cell hyperpolarizes below -50 mV.
   - At threshold (~ -40 mV), **Phase 0 Upstroke** occurs via the opening of **Voltage-Gated L-type Calcium Channels ($Ca_v1.2$)**, producing a slower upstroke compared to ventricular myocytes.
   - **Phase 3 Repolarization** occurs as L-type $Ca^{2+}$ channels close and delayed-rectifier $K^+$ channels ($I_{Kr}, I_{Ks}$) open, driving the cell back to its maximal diastolic potential (~ -65 mV), where HCN channels reactivate.
2. **Conduction Pathway:** The action potential travels across atrial internodal pathways, arriving at the **Atrioventricular (AV) Node** located in the inferior interatrial septum. The AV node imposes a crucial physiological delay (~100 milliseconds) characterized by slow, calcium-mediated conduction; this delay guarantees that atrial contraction and ventricular diastolic filling are completed before ventricular mechanical systole initiates.
3. **Ventricular Conduction System:** Leaving the AV node, the impulse accelerates rapidly (3 to 4 m/s) down the common **Bundle of His**, dividing into the Left Bundle Branch (which bifurcates into anterior and posterior fascicles) and the Right Bundle Branch, terminating in the extensive subendocardial **Purkinje Fiber Network**.
4. **Working Ventricular Myocyte Action Potential (Phases 0–4):**
   - *Phase 0 (Rapid Upstroke):* Triggered when excitation reaches -70 mV; voltage-gated fast $Na^+$ channels ($Na_v1.5$) open, producing massive inward $Na^+$ current that drives potential to +30 mV.
   - *Phase 1 (Early Repolarization):* Rapid inactivation of $Na_v$ channels and brief activation of transient outward potassium current ($I_{to}$).
   - *Phase 2 (Plateau Phase):* Unique to cardiac myocytes, lasting 200–300 ms. Inward flux of $Ca^{2+}$ through L-type calcium channels ($Ca_v1.2$) is electrically balanced by outward $K^+$ current ($I_{Ks}, I_{Kr}$). This $Ca^{2+}$ influx triggers massive **Calcium-Induced Calcium Release (CICR)** from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via ryanodine receptors (RyR2), activating sarcomeric troponin C to generate mechanical cross-bridge cycling.
   - *Phase 3 (Final Repolarization):* L-type $Ca^{2+}$ channels inactivate; delayed-rectifier $K^+$ currents dominate, restoring electrical negativity.
   - *Phase 4 (Resting State):* Maintained stably near the potassium equilibrium potential (-85 to -90 mV) by inward-rectifier potassium channels ($I_{K1}$).

## The Cardiac Cycle, Pressure Volumes, and the Wiggers Diagram

The **Cardiac Cycle** integrates electrical excitation, intracardiac pressure fluctuations, ventricular volume shifts, and valvular movements through two primary phases: **Diastole** (ventricular relaxation and filling) and **Systole** (ventricular contraction and ejection).

The sequence is mapped systematically on the **Wiggers Diagram**:
1. **Isovolumetric Contraction (Early Systole):** QRS complex on ECG triggers ventricular depolarization. Ventricular intra-cavity pressure exceeds atrial pressure, abruptly snapping the mitral and tricuspid valves shut (generating the **First Heart Sound, $S_1$**). Because ventricular pressure has not yet exceeded aortic (80 mmHg) or pulmonary (10 mmHg) pressure, the semilunar valves remain closed. All four valves are shut; ventricular volume remains constant while ventricular pressure rises precipitously.
2. **Rapid and Reduced Ventricular Ejection:** Left ventricular pressure surpasses 80 mmHg, forcing the aortic valve open. Ventricles contract forcefully, ejecting their **Stroke Volume (SV)** into the great vessels. Peak systolic pressure reaches ~120 mmHg in the left ventricle.
3. **Isovolumetric Relaxation (Early Diastole):** Ventricular repolarization (T wave on ECG) causes intraventricular pressure to fall below arterial pressure. Backflow catches the cusps of the aortic and pulmonary valves, snapping them shut (generating the **Second Heart Sound, $S_2$**). All valves are momentarily closed; pressure plummets while volume remains fixed at the **End-Systolic Volume (ESV)**.
4. **Ventricular Filling (Rapid, Reduced, and Atrial Systole):** Ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, forcing AV valves open. Rapid early passive filling occurs (responsible for the pathological $S_3$ heart sound in volume overload states). Terminal filling occurs when the SA node fires (P wave on ECG), triggering atrial mechanical contraction (the "atrial kick"), which contributes the final 15–20% of the **End-Diastolic Volume (EDV)**.

Fundamental Hemodynamic Calculations:
- **Stroke Volume (SV):** $SV = EDV - ESV$ (typically $120\text{ mL} - 50\text{ mL} = 70\text{ mL}$ at rest).
- **Ejection Fraction (EF):** $EF = \frac{SV}{EDV} \times 100\%$ (normal range: 55% to 70%).
- **Cardiac Output (CO):** $CO = \text{Heart Rate (HR)} \times SV$ (typically $72\text{ bpm} \times 70\text{ mL} \approx 5.0\text{ L/min}$ at rest).
- **Frank-Starling Law of the Heart:** Within physiological limits, the force of myocardial contraction is directly proportional to the initial resting length (stretch) of cardiac muscle fibers prior to contraction (preload). Increased venous return increases EDV, stretching sarcomeres toward their optimal actin-myosin overlap length (~2.2 $\mu$m), which increases stroke volume on the subsequent beat.

## Hemodynamics, Poiseuille's Law, and Vascular Resistance

The physical movement of blood obeys hydraulic principles of fluid dynamics, governed by Ohm's law of circulation:
$$\Delta P = Q \times R \quad \implies \quad \text{Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP)} - \text{Central Venous Pressure (CVP)} = \text{Cardiac Output (CO)} \times \text{Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)}$$
Because CVP is near zero (~2–6 mmHg), the equation simplifies to:
$$\text{MAP} \approx \text{CO} \times \text{SVR}$$
Clinically, MAP is estimated from cuff pressures: $\text{MAP} \approx \text{Diastolic BP} + \frac{1}{3}(\text{Systolic BP} - \text{Diastolic BP})$.

**Poiseuille's Law** defines the physical determinants of hydraulic vascular resistance ($R$):
$$R = \frac{8 \eta L}{\pi r^4}$$
where $\eta$ is dynamic blood viscosity (governed by hematocrit and plasma proteins), $L$ is total vascular length, and $r$ is the internal radius of the blood vessel.

Because resistance is inversely proportional to the **fourth power of the vessel radius ($r^4$)**, small variations in vascular caliber exert immense effects on regional blood flow and systemic arterial pressure:
- Halving a vessel's radius ($r \to 0.5r$) increases hydraulic resistance by a factor of sixteen ($2^4 = 16$).
- **Arterioles** possess a dense circumferential coat of vascular smooth muscle and contribute over 60% of total systemic vascular resistance. Consequently, arterioles function as the primary adjustable hemodynamic stopcocks of the circulation. Arteriolar tone is modulated by:
  - *Sympathetic Adrenergic Tone:* Norepinephrine binding $\alpha_1$ receptors causes vasoconstriction; epinephrine binding $\beta_2$ receptors in skeletal muscle beds causes vasodilation.
  - *Local Metabolic Autoregulation:* Working tissues release local vasodilators (adenosine, high $CO_2$, low $O_2$, $H^+$, lactate, $K^+$) that override sympathetic tone to increase local nutrient perfusion (active hyperemia).
  - *Endothelial Factors:* Endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) generates **Nitric Oxide (NO)**, which diffuses into vascular smooth muscle, activating soluble guanylyl cyclase to increase cGMP, causing potent vasodilation.

## Microcirculation and Capillary Starling Forces

Transcapillary fluid exchange between the intravascular vascular space and the interstitial fluid space is governed by **Starling's Principle of Fluid Exchange**:
$$J_v = K_f \left[ (P_c - P_i) - \sigma (\pi_c - \pi_i) \right]$$
where:
- $J_v$ is net transcapillary fluid filtration volume;
- $K_f$ is capillary hydraulic filtration coefficient (water permeability);
- $P_c$ is Capillary Hydrostatic Pressure (drives fluid outward into interstitium; ~30–35 mmHg at arteriolar end, ~15 mmHg at venular end);
- $P_i$ is Interstitial Fluid Hydrostatic Pressure (typically -2 to 0 mmHg in subcutaneous tissue);
- $\sigma$ is the reflection coefficient for plasma proteins (approximates 1.0 in non-fenestrated capillaries, indicating total impermeability to albumin);
- $\pi_c$ is Capillary Plasma Colloid Oncotic Pressure (~26–28 mmHg, exerted predominantly by dissolved serum **Albumin**; draws fluid inward into the capillary);
- $\pi_i$ is Interstitial Colloid Oncotic Pressure (~1–3 mmHg; draws fluid into the interstitium).

Across systemic capillary networks, total outward hydrostatic filtration slightly exceeds inward oncotic reabsorption by approximately 2 to 4 liters per day. This excess filtered fluid enters blind-ended **Lymphatic Capillaries**, flowing through regional lymph nodes and returning to the systemic circulation via the thoracic duct into the left subclavian vein. Failure of Starling equilibrium—through elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure (congestive heart failure), severe hypoalbuminemia (liver cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome), or lymphatic obstruction—leads directly to clinical **Edema**.

## Hemostasis: Platelet Plug Formation and the Coagulation Cascade

When vascular endothelial integrity is disrupted, the physiological response of **Hemostasis** arrests hemorrhage through three interdependent phases:

1. **Vascular Spasm:** Endothelial injury triggers immediate local myogenic reflex contraction and the release of **Endothelin-1**, transiently constricting the vessel lumen to reduce blood loss.
2. **Primary Hemostasis (Platelet Plug Formation):**
   - *Adhesion:* Endothelial denudation exposes subendothelial collagen. Circulating **von Willebrand Factor (vWF)** binds to exposed collagen; platelets bind to immobilized vWF via surface **Glycoprotein Ib (GPIb)** receptors.
   - *Activation:* Adherent platelets undergo shape change, extending pseudopodia, and exocytose cytoplasmic storage granules:
     - Dense granules release ADP (which binds platelet $P2Y_{12}$ receptors) and serotonin (vasoconstriction).
     - Alpha granules release fibrinogen, fibronectin, and Factor V.
     - Platelet membrane phospholipase $A_2$ synthesizes **Thromboxane $A_2$ ($TXA_2$)**, a potent platelet activator and vasoconstrictor (antagonized by low-dose aspirin, which irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase-1).
   - *Aggregation:* ADP and $TXA_2$ induce a conformational change in platelet **Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa)** receptors, exposing binding pockets for dimeric plasma **Fibrinogen**. Fibrinogen cross-links adjacent platelets, forming an unstable, primary hemostatic platelet plug.
3. **Secondary Hemostasis (The Coagulation Cascade):**
   A series of amplifying enzymatic cleavages converting soluble circulating zymogens into active serine proteases, culminating in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into an insoluble, cross-linked **Fibrin Mesh**:
   - *Extrinsic Pathway (Physiological Initiator):* Subendothelial perivascular cells expose **Tissue Factor (TF / Factor III)** to circulating blood, complexing with activated **Factor VIIa** in the presence of $Ca^{2+}$. This TF-VIIa complex directly activates Factor X.
   - *Intrinsic Pathway (Amplification Loop):* Initiated when Factor XII contacts negatively charged surfaces, sequentially activating Factor XI, Factor IX, and forming the tenase complex (Factor IXa, Factor VIIIa, $Ca^{2+}$, phospholipids), which strongly activates Factor X.
   - *Common Pathway:* Factor Xa complexes with activated Factor Va, $Ca^{2+}$, and platelet acidic phospholipids to assemble the **Prothrombinase Complex**. Prothrombinase cleaves **Prothrombin (Factor II)** into active **Thrombin (Factor IIa)**.
   - *Fibrin Formation:* Thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptides A and B from **Fibrinogen (Factor I)**, producing insoluble fibrin monomers that polymerize spontaneously. Thrombin simultaneously activates **Factor XIII (Fibrin-Stabilizing Factor)**, a transglutaminase that forms covalent isopeptide cross-links between fibrin polymers, consolidating the plug into a permanent hemostatic clot.
