# Digestive and Renal Excretory Systems: Gastrointestinal Absorption, Nephron Kinetics, and Acid-Base Regulation

## Histological Architecture and Motility of the Gastrointestinal Tract

The human gastrointestinal (GI) tract forms a continuous muscular tube extending from the oral cavity to the anal canal, structured into four concentric histological layers: the inner **Mucosa** (epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae), **Submucosa** (containing the Meissner/submucosal plexus governing glandular secretion), **Muscularis Externa** (inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle layers coordinated by the Auerbach/myenteric plexus governing peristalsis), and the outer **Serosa or Adventitia**.

Salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual) secrete saliva containing **Salivary $\alpha$-Amylase** (initiating the hydrolysis of dietary starch into maltose and $\alpha$-limit dextrins at neutral pH) and **Lingual Lipase**. Following mastication, the tongue pushes the food bolus into the oropharynx, initiating involuntary swallowing reflexes mediated by the swallowing center in the medulla oblongata, which relaxes the Upper Esophageal Sphincter (UES) and drives primary peristaltic waves down the esophagus toward the Lower Esophageal Sphincter (LES).

## Gastric Secretory Cell Lineages and Acid Production Kinetics

The stomach acts as an acid reservoir and mechanical grinder. The oxyntic glands within the gastric body and fundus contain specialized secretory cell lineages:
- **Parietal (Oxyntic) Cells:** Secrete **Hydrochloric Acid ($HCl$)** up to a concentration of 150 mmol/L (pH 0.8–1.5). The apical membrane expresses the primary active **$H^+/K^+$-ATPase (Proton Pump)**, which exports $H^+$ into the gastric lumen in exchange for incoming $K^+$. Parietal acid secretion is stimulated by three convergent pathways: (1) **Histamine** (secreted by Enterochromaffin-Like / ECL cells, acting on $G_s$-coupled $H_2$ receptors to raise cAMP), (2) **Gastrin** (secreted by antral G cells into the bloodstream, acting on $G_q$-coupled $CCK_B$ receptors to raise intracellular $Ca^{2+}$), and (3) **Acetylcholine** (released by postganglionic vagal parasympathetic fibers, acting on $G_q$-coupled $M_3$ receptors). Somatostatin (from D cells) and prostaglandins ($PGE_2$) inhibit acid secretion via $G_i$. Parietal cells simultaneously secrete **Intrinsic Factor**, an essential glycoprotein that binds dietary Vitamin $B_{12}$ (cobalamin) to permit its downstream receptor-mediated endocytosis in the terminal ileum.
- **Chief (Zymogenic) Cells:** Synthesize and secrete **Pepsinogen**, an inactive proenzyme. Exposure to gastric acid causes autocatalytic cleavage of pepsinogen into active **Pepsin**, an endopeptidase optimal at pH 1.5 to 2.0 that cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic amino acids.
- **Mucous Neck Cells:** Secrete an alkaline, bicarbonate-rich mucus gel layer (~0.2 mm thick) that adheres to the gastric mucosa, establishing a protective pH gradient (pH 7.0 at the cell surface versus pH 1.5 in the gastric lumen) that prevents autodigestion by luminal acid and pepsin.

## Pancreatic Exocrine Secretions and Biliary Lipid Emulsification

Chyme entering the duodenum stimulates coordinated exocrine secretions from the pancreas and biliary system:

- **The Exocrine Pancreas:**
  - *Ductal Cells:* Secrete a high-volume, watery fluid containing up to 140 mmol/L of **Bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$)**, driven by the apical $Cl^-/HCO_3^-$ exchanger (pendrin) paired with the Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR) chloride channel. Ductal secretion is stimulated by **Secretin**, a peptide released by duodenal S cells in response to luminal acid (pH $< 4.5$). This bicarbonate neutralizes acidic gastric chyme in the duodenum to a neutral pH (7.0–8.0), which is mandatory for the activity of pancreatic enzymes.
  - *Acinar Cells:* Secrete hydrolytic digestive zymogens stimulated by **Cholecystokinin (CCK)** (released by duodenal I cells in response to luminal peptides and fatty acids). Acinar secretions include:
    - *Proteases:* **Trypsinogen**, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, and procarboxypeptidases A/B. The duodenal brush border transmembrane enzyme **Enteropeptidase (Enterokinase)** cleaves trypsinogen into active **Trypsin**. Trypsin autocatalytically cleaves all other pancreatic zymogens into their active, destructive forms.
    - *Pancreatic $\alpha$-Amylase:* Hydrolyzes internal $\alpha(1\to 4)$ glucosidic bonds of polysaccharides.
    - *Pancreatic Lipase and Colipase:* Hydrolyzes triglycerides into free fatty acids and 2-monoacylglycerol.
- **Biliary Secretion and Fat Emulsification:** The liver synthesizes amphipathic **Bile Salts** (cholic and chenodeoxycholic acids conjugated to glycine or taurine), which are stored and concentrated five- to ten-fold within the gallbladder. Duodenal CCK induces gallbladder smooth muscle contraction and sphincter of Oddi relaxation. Bile salts intersperse between lipid droplets, mechanically reducing their surface tension and breaking them into tiny emulsion droplets, providing a massive surface area for pancreatic lipase. Hydrolyzed lipid products associate with bile salts to form polymolecular **Micelles** (diameter 4–7 nm).

## Intestinal Epithelial Absorption and Enterohepatic Circulation

Nutrient absorption in the small intestine relies on specialized brush border enterocyte transport systems:

- *Carbohydrates:* Brush border disaccharidases (lactase, sucrase, maltase) cleave oligosaccharides into absorbable monosaccharides. Glucose and galactose are transported across the enterocyte apical membrane via the secondary active **SGLT1 Symporter** (driven by the $Na^+$ gradient). Fructose is transported down its concentration gradient via facilitated diffusion through **GLUT5**. All three monosaccharides exit the basolateral membrane into the portal circulation via **GLUT2**.
- *Proteins:* Luminal amino acids are absorbed via $Na^+$-dependent amino acid symporters; di- and tripeptides are absorbed via the $H^+$-dependent **PepT1** oligopeptide symporter, hydrolyzed into single amino acids by cytoplasmic peptidases, and exported across the basolateral membrane into portal capillaries.
- *Lipids:* Micelles diffuse through the unstirred aqueous layer to the apical microvilli; free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols diffuse passively across the enterocyte membrane or enter via CD36. Inside the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, they are re-esterified into triglycerides and packaged with apolipoproteins (predominantly **ApoB-48**), cholesterol, and phospholipids into spherical **Chylomicrons** (diameter 75–450 nm). Chylomicrons are exocytosed across the basolateral membrane into blind-ended lymphatic **Lacteals**, traveling via the thoracic duct directly into the systemic venous circulation, completely bypassing first-pass hepatic metabolism.
- *The Terminal Ileum:* Reabsorbs $>95\%$ of conjugated bile salts via the Apical Sodium-Dependent Bile Acid Transporter (ASBT), returning them to the liver via the portal vein (**Enterohepatic Circulation**). The terminal ileum also mediates the endocytosis of Vitamin $B_{12}$-Intrinsic Factor complexes via cubilin-amnionless receptors.

## Glomerular Micro-Architecture and the Three-Layer Filtration Barrier

The human kidneys receive approximately 20% to 25% of resting cardiac output (~1.0 to 1.2 L/min of renal blood flow / RBF) via the renal arteries, filtering approximately 180 liters of plasma daily across two million functional subunits termed **Nephrons**.

High-pressure capillary tufts (the glomerulus) encased within Bowman's capsule filter plasma across a three-layered molecular sieve:
1. *Fenestrated Capillary Endothelium:* Large circular pores measuring 70 to 100 nm in diameter; blocks cellular blood elements (erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets) while permitting fluid and proteins to pass.
2. *Glomerular Basement Membrane (GBM):* A thick (~300 nm) acellular matrix composed of type IV collagen, laminin, entactin, and negatively charged **Heparan Sulfate Proteoglycans**. The negative electrostatic charge repels negatively charged circulating plasma proteins (especially serum albumin, diameter ~3.6 nm), preventing their filtration.
3. *Visceral Epithelial Layer (Podocytes):* Specialized cells that extend primary and interdigitating secondary foot processes (**Pedicels**) wrapping around glomerular capillaries. Between adjacent pedicels lie **Filtration Slits** (width 4 to 14 nm) bridged by an isoporous protein diaphragm containing **Nephrin** and **Podocin**.

Filtration across the barrier is restricted strictly by molecular size ($< 4\text{ nm}$ or $< 70\text{ kDa}$ filtered freely; $> 8\text{ nm}$ completely excluded) and negative charge (neutral molecules filter much more readily than polyanionic species of identical hydrodynamic radius).

## Glomerular Filtration Rate and Hemodynamic Starling Forces

Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is the volume of fluid filtered into Bowman's capsule per unit time, typically **125 mL/min** (~180 L/day in an adult male). GFR is dictated by the net Starling pressure across the glomerular capillary wall:

$$\text{GFR} = K_f \left[ (P_{GC} - P_{BS}) - \sigma (\pi_{GC} - \pi_{BS}) \right]$$

where:
- $K_f$ is the glomerular ultrafiltration coefficient (a product of hydraulic conductivity and total capillary surface area);
- $P_{GC}$ is glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (~55–60 mmHg, kept uniquely high because the capillary bed is bounded by both an afferent and an efferent arteriole);
- $P_{BS}$ is Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure (~15 mmHg);
- $\pi_{GC}$ is glomerular oncotic pressure (~30 mmHg);
- $\pi_{BS}$ is Bowman's space oncotic pressure (zero under normal conditions lacking filtered protein);
- $\sigma$ is the reflection coefficient for plasma proteins (~1.0).

Net ultrafiltration pressure is approximately $+10\text{ to }+15\text{ mmHg}$. Autoregulation of afferent and efferent arteriolar resistance by myogenic mechanisms and tubuloglomerular feedback maintains both RBF and GFR constant across mean arterial pressures ranging from 80 to 180 mmHg.

## Proximal Convoluted Tubule Transport Kinetics

The 180 liters of primary ultrafiltrate entering the tubular lumen undergo extensive reabsorption in the **Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)**:
- Reabsorbs approximately **65% of filtered water, sodium, and chloride**.
- Reabsorbs **100% of filtered glucose and amino acids** via apical secondary active symporters (SGLT2 in early PCT segments, SGLT1 in late segments; saturated when blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold of ~180–200 mg/dL, causing glucosuria).
- Reabsorbs **85% to 90% of filtered bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$)**: Apical $Na^+/H^+$ antiporters (NHE3) secrete $H^+$ into the lumen. Luminal $H^+$ reacts with filtered $HCO_3^-$ to form $H_2CO_3$. Membrane-bound **Carbonic Anhydrase IV** cleaves $H_2CO_3$ into water and lipid-soluble $CO_2$, which diffuses rapidly across the apical membrane into the tubular cell. Intracellular **Carbonic Anhydrase II** re-hydrates $CO_2$ back into $H^+$ and $HCO_3^-$. The regenerated $HCO_3^-$ is exported across the basolateral membrane into the blood via the electrogenic $Na^+/3HCO_3^-$ cotransporter (NBCe1).
- Because water follows solute reabsorption isotonically through high-density Aquaporin-1 channels, the tubular fluid remaining at the end of the PCT is **isosmotic** to plasma (~300 mOsm/L).

## Loop of Henle and Countercurrent Multiplication

The Loop of Henle establishes the hypertonic medullary interstitial osmotic gradient (ranging from 300 mOsm/L at the corticomedullary junction up to **1200 mOsm/L** in the deep inner renal medulla):
- *Thin Descending Limb:* Highly permeable to water via constitutive Aquaporin-1 channels, but virtually impermeable to $NaCl$ and urea. As fluid descends into the increasingly hypertonic medulla, water exits into the interstitium, concentrating tubular fluid to 1200 mOsm/L at the hairpin turn.
- *Thin Ascending Limb:* Impermeable to water; moderately permeable to $NaCl$, which diffuses passively down its concentration gradient out of the lumen into the interstitium.
- *Thick Ascending Limb (TAL):* Completely impermeable to water. Actively translocates solutes from the tubular lumen into the medullary interstitium via the apical **$Na^+$-$K^+$-$2Cl^-$ Cotransporter (NKCC2)** (the pharmacologic target of loop diuretics such as furosemide). Back-leak of $K^+$ through apical ROMK channels creates a lumen-positive transepithelial potential (+8 to +10 mV), driving the paracellular reabsorption of divalent cations ($Ca^{2+}, Mg^{2+}$). Because solutes are actively pumped out without water, the fluid exiting the TAL is **hypoosmotic (~100 mOsm/L)**, establishing its designation as the "diluting segment."
- *The Vasa Recta:* Specialized hairpin capillary loops originating from efferent arterioles of juxtamedullary nephrons that descend into the inner medulla. They act as **Countercurrent Exchangers**, maintaining the medullary osmotic gradient by operating with slow blood flow that permits passive solute-water equilibration without washing out the medullary interstitial hypertonicity.

## Distal Tubule and Collecting Duct Regulation

Tubular processing in the distal nephron executes the fine-tuning of electrolyte balance and urine volume:
1. **Early Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT):**
   - Impermeable to water; reabsorbs approximately 5% of filtered $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$ via the apical electroneutral **$Na^+/Cl^-$ Cotransporter (NCC)** (the pharmacologic target of thiazide diuretics).
   - Active site of parathyroid hormone (PTH)-regulated calcium reabsorption via apical TRPV5 channels and basolateral NCX exchangers.
2. **Late Distal Tubule and Cortical Collecting Duct:**
   Possesses two functionally distinct, specialized cell lineages:
   - **Principal Cells:** Reabsorb $Na^+$ from the lumen down an electrical gradient via apical **Epithelial Sodium Channels (ENaC)** (inhibited by potassium-sparing diuretics such as amiloride). The reabsorption of $Na^+$ creates a lumen-negative transepithelial voltage that drives the passive excretion of $K^+$ into the lumen through apical ROMK and Maxi-K channels. Principal cells are the target of **Aldosterone** (which upregulates basolateral $Na^+/K^+$-ATPase pumps and apical ENaC channels) and **Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)** (which drives the exocytic insertion of **Aquaporin-2** water channels into the apical membrane, permitting water to be drawn out of the hypoosmotic lumen into the hypertonic medullary interstitium to produce concentrated urine).
   - **Intercalated Cells:** Specialized for acid-base homeostasis:
     - *$\alpha$-Intercalated Cells:* Active during metabolic acidosis; secrete $H^+$ into the lumen via apical electrogenic $H^+$-ATPases and $H^+/K^+$-ATPases, and export newly generated $HCO_3^-$ into systemic blood via basolateral anion exchangers (AE1).
     - *$\beta$-Intercalated Cells:* Active during metabolic alkalosis; invert this polarity, expressing apical pendrin ($Cl^-/HCO_3^-$ exchangers) to excrete excess $HCO_3^-$ into the urine.

## Renal Bicarbonate Reclamation and Carbonic Anhydrase Kinetics

Systemic arterial pH is defended within the strict physiological range of **7.35 to 7.45** ($[H^+] \approx 35\text{ to }45\text{ nmol/L}$). Cellular protein catabolism and dietary metabolism produce approximately 50 to 100 mmol of non-volatile (fixed) metabolic acids per day (sulfuric acid from methionine/cysteine; phosphoric acid from phospholipids; lactic acid; ketoacids), which cannot be exhaled by the lungs.

The kidneys defend systemic acid-base balance first through complete reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate:
- The kidneys filter approximately 4320 mmol of $HCO_3^-$ per day ($180\text{ L/day} \times 24\text{ mmol/L}$).
- Over 99.9% of this filtered load must be reabsorbed to prevent fatal metabolic acidosis, occurring predominantly (85–90%) in the proximal convoluted tubule via apical NHE3 proton secretion and membrane-bound carbonic anhydrase IV, coupled to basolateral NBCe1 cotransport.
- The remaining filtered bicarbonate is reclaimed in the thick ascending limb and the distal nephron intercalated cells.

## Generation of New Bicarbonate: Titratable Acids and Ammoniagenesis

To replace bicarbonate consumed in buffering daily metabolic acid production, the kidneys generate new bicarbonate through two complementary excretory mechanisms:

1. **Excretion of Fixed Acids via Titratable Acid Buffer Systems:** Hydrogen ions secreted into the tubular lumen by $\alpha$-intercalated cells are buffered by filtered inorganic phosphate:
   $$HPO_4^{2-} \text{ (Monohydrogen Phosphate)} + H^+ \to H_2PO_4^- \text{ (Dihydrogen Phosphate)}$$
   $H_2PO_4^-$ is trapped within the tubular lumen and excreted in urine as "titratable acid," contributing approximately 10 to 30 mmol of net acid excretion per day while generating an equimolar quantity of new $HCO_3^-$ that enters systemic blood.
2. **Ammoniagenesis and Ammonium ($NH_4^+$) Excretion:** Represents the primary adjustable mechanism for eliminating large acid loads. Proximal tubule enterocytes metabolize the amino acid **Glutamine** into two molecules of ammonium ($NH_4^+$) and two molecules of new bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$):
   $$\text{Glutamine} \to 2 NH_4^+ + 2 HCO_3^-$$
   The newly synthesized $HCO_3^-$ is exported into the peritubular capillary blood. The $NH_4^+$ is secreted into the tubular lumen via the apical $Na^+/H^+$ exchanger (NHE3, substituting $NH_4^+$ for $H^+$), reabsorbed in the thick ascending limb, concentrated in the medullary interstitium as non-ionized ammonia ($NH_3$), and finally diffuses into the medullary collecting duct lumen where it traps secreted $H^+$ to form non-diffusible $NH_4^+$, which is excreted in the urine. Ammoniagenesis can increase up to ten-fold (eliminating $>300\text{ mmol } H^+/\text{day}$) during chronic systemic metabolic acidosis.
