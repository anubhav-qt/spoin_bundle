## The Dual Nature of Ishvara: Apara and Para Prakriti (BG 7.4–5)
In Chapter 7, verses 4 and 5, Krishna articulates the ontological structure of the universe by distinguishing between two expressions of divine nature (*Prakriti*). In verse 7.4, Krishna details his lower, material nature (*Apara Prakriti*), which consists of eight constituent principles: the five foundational elements of physical nature—Earth (*Bhumi*), Water (*Apas*), Fire (*Anala*), Air (*Vayu*), and Space/Ether (*Kham*)—along with the psychological and cognitive faculties of Mind (*Manas*), Discernment/Intellect (*Buddhi*), and Ego-individuation (*Ahamkara*). This eightfold lower nature represents the insentient, mutable, objective fabric of the phenomenal cosmos. In verse 7.5, Krishna introduces his higher, spiritual nature (*Para Prakriti*): "Beyond this lower nature, know My other, higher nature, which comprises the life-element (*Jiva-bhutam*) by which this entire universe is sustained and animated" (*Apareyam itas tv anyam prakritim viddhi me param*). Through this formulation, the Gita bridges the gap between matter and consciousness: the material universe is not an independent, self-existent mechanistic reality, but an objective projection sustained and infused at every level by divine conscious energy.

## The Field and the Knower: Kshetra and Kshetrajna (BG 13.1–6)
Chapter 13 formalizes this ontological division into the epistemological categories of the Field (*Kshetra*) and the Knower of the Field (*Kshetrajna*). In verse 13.1, Krishna states: "This body, O son of Kunti, is called the Field; and whoever knows it is called the Knower of the Field by the seers of truth" (*Idam shariram kaunteya kshetram ity abhidhiyate*). Verses 13.5 and 13.6 enumerate the full structural composition of the Field: the twenty-four cosmic principles of classical Samkhya philosophy—the unmanifest causal matter (*Avyakta*), intellect (*Buddhi*), ego-identity (*Ahamkara*), mind (*Manas*), the five sense organs of perception, the five organs of motor action, the five subtle sensory objects—along with all psychological phenomena, including desire (*Ichha*), aversion (*Dvesha*), pleasure (*Sukha*), pain (*Duhkha*), conscious cohesion (*Sanghata*), and resolve (*Dhriti*). All thoughts, emotions, sensations, and bodily alterations belong strictly to the objective Field. The *Kshetrajna*, by contrast, is the pure, immutable witnessing consciousness that perceives the Field without being physically constituted by it. In verse 13.2, Krishna reveals the ultimate identity of this witness: "Know Me also as the Knower of the Field in all fields, O Bharata" (*Kshetrajnam chapi mam viddhi sarva-kshetreshu bharata*). The conscious witness within the individual is non-different from the supreme divine consciousness pervading all existence.

## The Veil of Maya and the Guna Matrix (BG 7.14)
The Gita explains the apparent separation between the individual soul and the Divine through the concept of *Maya*—the divine power of cosmic illusion. In Chapter 7, verse 14, Krishna declares: "Verily, this divine illusion of Mine, constituted of the three gunas, is extraordinarily difficult to cross beyond; but those who surrender unto Me alone cross beyond this illusion" (*Daivi hy esha guna-mayi mama maya duratyaya / mam eva ye prapadyante mayam etam taranti te*). *Maya* is not non-existence or absolute voidness, but the creative projecting power (*Vikshepa-shakti*) and concealing power (*Avarana-shakti*) of the Divine. Through the interplay of the three qualities of nature (*Sattva*, *Rajas*, and *Tamas*), Maya veils the underlying non-dual unity of Brahman, projecting the vivid appearance of an independent material world populated by separate, competing entities. Under the spell of Maya, the conscious Self mistakenly identifies with the physical body and psychological ego, becoming trapped in the cycle of craving, grief, and rebirth (*Samsara*). Liberation occurs when the seeker sees through this cosmic projection by recognizing their true nature as transcendent consciousness and aligning their will with the Supreme.

## The Inverted Cosmic Tree: Ashvattha (BG 15.1–4)
Chapter 15 opens with one of the most striking metaphysical allegories in world literature: the inverted cosmic sacred fig tree (*Ashvattha*). In verse 15.1, Krishna describes: "With roots above and branches below, the imperishable Ashvattha tree is spoken of, whose leaves are the sacred Vedic hymns; one who truly understands this tree knows the essence of the Vedas" (*Urdhva-mulam adhah-shakham ashvattham prahur avyayam*). Unlike terrestrial trees, the cosmic tree of phenomenal existence has its ultimate root above in the unmanifest, transcendent Reality (*Brahman*). Its trunk and branches extend downward into the manifest material worlds, nourished by the three *Gunas*, with sensory objects forming its sprouting leaves and buds. Its secondary root systems reach downward into human consciousness, binding individuals to earthly action through desire and karma. In verses 15.3 and 15.4, Krishna warns that the true beginning, end, and structural foundation of this cosmic entanglement cannot be perceived by the ordinary worldly mind. To attain liberation, the spiritual aspirant must take the sharpened "axe of non-attachment" (*Asanga-shastrena dridhena chittva*) and resolutely sever the tree of worldly clinging, seeking that primal, supreme foundation from which there is no return to bondage.

## The Triad of Realities: Kshara, Akshara, and Purushottama (BG 15.16–18)
In verses 15.16 through 15.18, the Gita presents the culmination of its metaphysical hierarchy by synthesizing dualism and non-dualism into the doctrine of the Supreme Person (*Purushottama*). Verse 15.16 classifies all realities in the universe into two categories: the Perishable (*Kshara*) and the Imperishable (*Akshara*). *Kshara* encompasses all manifest forms, material structures, physical bodies, and mutable cosmic elements (*Ksharah sarvani bhutani*). *Akshara* refers to the unaltering, immutable spiritual consciousness, often designated as the steadfast witness (*Kuta-stha*). In verse 15.17, Krishna reveals a third, supreme dimension that transcends both: "Distinct from both is the Supreme Person, proclaimed as the Paramatman (the Supreme Self), who pervades and sustains the three worlds as the inexhaustible Lord" (*Uttamah purushas tv anyah paramatmety udahritah*). In verse 15.18, Krishna explicitly confirms: "Because I transcend the perishable, and am higher even than the imperishable, I am celebrated in the world and in the sacred Vedas as the Purushottama" (*Yasmāt ksharam atito 'ham aksharad api cottamah / ato 'smi loke vede ca prathitah purushottamah*). Classical commentators note that this doctrine provides an all-inclusive ontological framework: while Ramanuja interprets Purushottama as the personal Supreme Deity possessing infinite auspicious attributes, and Shankara interprets Purushottama as the non-dual absolute Brahman beyond relative phenomenal categories, both agree that Purushottama represents the ultimate ground of all existence.
