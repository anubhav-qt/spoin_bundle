## Empirical Pre-training Scaling Laws: Kaplan, Chinchilla, and Over-Training
The development of modern generative foundation models is guided by empirical scaling laws that predict model performance as a power-law function of compute, data, and parameters. In 2020, Jared Kaplan and OpenAI researchers established that cross-entropy validation loss scales predictably according to power laws: $L(N) \propto N^{-\alpha_N}$, $L(D) \propto D^{-\alpha_D}$, and $L(C) \propto C^{-\alpha_C}$. However, early interpretations favored scaling parameter count $N$ disproportionately faster than dataset size $D$, leading to massively under-trained models like GPT-3 (175 billion parameters trained on only 300 billion tokens). In 2022, Jordan Hoffmann and DeepMind revised these laws through the Chinchilla framework, proving that for compute-optimal training, parameter count and token volume must scale in equal proportions: $N \propto C^{0.5}$ and $D \propto C^{0.5}$, establishing an optimal ratio of roughly 20 tokens per model parameter (e.g., a 70B model requires 1.4 trillion tokens). In the current era, frontier labs deliberately violate compute-optimal ratios through "inference-optimal over-training." Exemplified by Meta's Llama 3 (training an 8B model on over 15 trillion tokens), investing massive compute during pre-training compresses immense world knowledge into compact architectures, dramatically reducing the per-token inference serving costs across billions of downstream user queries.

## Mixture of Experts (MoE) Architecture and Sparse Routing
As dense foundational models approached trillions of floating-point operations (FLOPs) per forward pass, scaling dense architectures encountered severe hardware power and thermal constraints: doubling dense parameters directly doubles computational cost per generated token. Mixture of Experts (MoE) resolves this bottleneck by decoupling a model's total parameter capacity from its per-token active compute. In an MoE architecture, the standard dense Feed-Forward Network (FFN) in each Transformer layer is replaced by a bank of $E$ independent Expert FFN networks. For every incoming token activation vector $\mathbf{x}$, a lightweight parameterized Gating Router computes a routing probability distribution: $g(\mathbf{x}) = \text{Softmax}(\text{TopK}(W_g \mathbf{x}, k))$. The token is dispatched exclusively to the $k$ highest-scoring experts (typically $k=2$ or $k=8$), and the final layer output is computed as the linearly weighted sum of the selected expert outputs: $\mathbf{y} = \sum_{i \in \text{TopK}} g_i(\mathbf{x}) \text{Expert}_i(\mathbf{x})$. By activating only a sparse subset of parameters per token (e.g., activating 37B parameters out of a 671B total capacity), MoE models achieve the representational capacity of a massive model while operating at the latency and computational cost of a fractionally sized dense model.

## Advanced MoE Engineering: DeepSeek-V3 and Auxiliary-Loss-Free Balancing
Early MoE implementations suffered from router collapse, where gating networks fell into local minima, routing the vast majority of tokens to a few popular experts while leaving others completely idle. Traditional mitigation relied on auxiliary load-balancing loss penalties, which artificially forced equal token distribution across experts; however, penalizing the routing objective directly degraded language modeling loss. DeepSeek-V3 introduced a landmark breakthrough with **Auxiliary-Loss-Free Load Balancing**. Instead of adding a penalty loss, the DeepSeek router introduces a dynamic bias term $b_i$ to each expert's affinity score: $s_{i,t} = \text{Sigmoid}(\mathbf{x}_t^T \mathbf{e}_i) + b_i$. If an expert becomes overloaded during a training step, its bias term is systematically decremented; if an expert is underutilized, its bias is incremented. This dynamic calibration ensures perfect hardware load balancing across GPU clusters without corrupting the primary cross-entropy training objective. Furthermore, DeepSeek-V3 pioneered **Fine-Grained Expert Division**, employing 256 routed experts alongside 1 dedicated Shared Expert per layer, routing each token to 8 specialized experts. The shared expert is unconditionally activated for every token, capturing universal linguistic invariants, while fine-grained routed experts develop exquisite specializations across diverse domains.

## Distributed Training Infrastructure and 3D Parallelism
Pre-training modern frontier models requires vast computing clusters comprising tens of thousands of specialized AI accelerators (such as NVIDIA H100, H200, B200 GPUs or Google TPU v5p pods) communicating over multi-terabit optical interconnects (NVLink 5.0 and 800 Gbps InfiniBand). Because a trillion-parameter model exceeds the physical memory capacity of any individual accelerator, training relies on 3D Parallelism—the composition of three complementary distributed execution paradigms. **Tensor Parallelism** (pioneered in Megatron-LM) shards individual matrix multiplications (such as query-key projections and FFN layers) across GPUs within a single high-bandwidth NVLink server chassis, executing intra-layer splits with all-reduce operations. **Pipeline Parallelism** partitions sequential Transformer layers across multiple server nodes, passing activations forward and gradients backward using 1F1B (One Forward, One Backward) scheduling algorithms to minimize pipeline bubbles. **Data Parallelism** duplicates the model across multiple nodes, synchronizing gradients across batches. To eliminate memory redundancy in data parallelism, the ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer) family shards optimizer states (ZeRO-1), gradients (ZeRO-2), and model parameters (ZeRO-3 / FSDP) across the entire cluster, reconstituting weights on the fly during forward and backward passes. In MoE training, **Expert Parallelism** distributes expert networks across disjoint nodes, coordinating token routing via high-speed All-to-All network primitives.

## Mixed Precision Computing: The Shift to Native FP8 and FP4
The computational throughput of frontier generative AI has been fundamentally transformed by the evolution of low-precision numerical formats in GPU Tensor Cores. Historically, training relied on single-precision FP32 (32-bit floating point), transitioning in 2020 to mixed-precision FP16 and BF16 (Bfloat16), which slashed VRAM footprint and doubled matrix-multiplication throughput. Frontier architectures like DeepSeek-V3 established the viability of training massive 600B+ parameter models from scratch using 8-bit floating point (**FP8**). FP8 standardizes two distinct representations: E4M3 (1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits) for forward activations and weights, maximizing precision for numerical calculations, and E5M2 (1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, 2 mantissa bits) for backward gradients, providing wider dynamic range to prevent gradient underflow. Native FP8 halves memory bandwidth consumption and doubles compute density while retaining FP32 master weights for numerical stability. In contemporary 2025/2026 architectures, NVIDIA's Blackwell (B200/GB200) platform introduces second-generation Transformer Engines supporting native **FP4** (4-bit floating point) Tensor Cores, delivering an additional $2\times$ to $4\times$ leap in compute density and running trillion-parameter MoE models at unprecedented scale and efficiency.
